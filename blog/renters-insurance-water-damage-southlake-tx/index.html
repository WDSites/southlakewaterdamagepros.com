Renters Insurance Water Damage in Southlake, TX: Who Pays for What

A plain guide for tenants who woke up to a leak and are not sure whether to call the landlord, the insurer, or both.

☎ Call (817) 623-9267

By the Southlake Water Damage Pros team · Updated 2026

Renters insurance water damage claims start with one confusing question: whose problem is the wet floor, yours or the landlord's? In Southlake, TX the honest answer is that it depends on what got wet, what caused it, and what your lease and policy say.

Renters insurance is a policy that protects a tenant's belongings and some living costs, while the building itself is normally the owner's responsibility. This guide walks through how those two sides split up, what to photograph before anything is moved, and why the first phone call usually goes to your landlord.

Tenant responsibilities and landlord responsibilities

Think of a rental as two separate piles of property. The walls, floors, ceilings, plumbing, and appliances that came with the unit belong to the owner. Your couch, clothes, laptop, bed, and rugs belong to you.

That split drives almost every decision after a leak. The landlord's property insurance, if there is a policy, is meant for the building. Your renters policy is meant for your things, and often for the cost of living somewhere else if the unit cannot be used.

Your lease is the document that settles the gray areas, such as who calls a plumber, who hires a drying company, and who must be told first. Read the water, repair, and notice clauses tonight if you can reach the lease. If you cannot find it, ask the property manager for a copy in writing.

Contents versus structure: what each policy is built for

Most Texas homeowner and renters policies cover sudden and accidental water damage, according to the Texas Department of Insurance. A burst supply line, an overflowing toilet, or a failed washer hose are the usual examples. Gradual leaks and slow seepage are generally not covered, and neither is the mold they cause.

The table below shows the usual dividing line. Treat it as a starting point and ask your insurer how your own policy reads, because wording differs from one policy to the next.

Sofa, mattress, clothing, electronicsYes, a renters policy is built for theseNo
Carpet, flooring, drywall that came with the unitRarely, unless the lease says otherwiseYes, building repair
Supply line, drain, water heater, built-in appliancesOnly if the tenant caused the damageYes, repair and replacement
Hotel or temporary housing while the unit is unusablePossibly, if the policy includes living expensesDepends on the lease
Mold that grew on a covered, damaged itemPossibly, covered with the itemDepends on the cause

Where the tenant caused the leak, such as an overflowing tub, the landlord's insurer may look to the tenant's liability coverage. Ask your insurer whether your policy includes it before you assume anything.

What to document before anything is moved

Insurers decide claims on evidence, and the evidence disappears fast when wet items go to the curb. TDI advises policyholders to take photos or video before repairs begin and to keep damaged items until the adjuster has seen them.

Start with wide shots of each wet room, then move closer to the water line on walls, the stain on a ceiling, and any item that is ruined. Narrate the video out loud with the date and what you see. Photograph serial numbers on electronics too.

  • Photograph the source if you can see it, such as a split hose or a dripping fixture.
  • Write down the time you noticed the water and the time you shut it off.
  • Make a list of damaged belongings with brand, age, and a rough replacement cost.
  • Keep receipts for towels, fans, hotel nights, and anything else you buy because of the leak.
  • Save every text and email with the landlord or property manager.

Why you should call the landlord first

In most leases the tenant must report a leak promptly, and the landlord controls access to the shutoff valves, the water heater closet, and the walls where pipes run. Waiting a day to tell them can hurt a later claim and let water spread into the unit next door.

So the order is simple. Stop the water if you safely can, then call or text the landlord, then call your insurer. Ask the landlord who will arrange emergency drying, because a restoration company working in a rental needs the owner's permission before opening walls or lifting floors.

If you cannot reach anyone and water keeps spreading, you can still call a restoration team to begin emergency water removal and extract standing water. Tell the owner what you did as soon as you reach them.

What drying looks like in a rented unit

A restoration crew starts with moisture meters and a thermal imaging camera to find where water traveled, since it often runs behind baseboards and under flooring that looks dry. Air movers and LGR dehumidifiers then run for days, and the crew rechecks readings until the materials return to a normal level.

That work falls under water mitigation, and the structural part of it is normally arranged by the owner or manager. Your belongings need separate attention. Wet clothing and soft goods can be pulled out early, while electronics and papers should be set aside for the insurer to see.

We do not rent equipment to tenants, and renting a fan from a hardware store rarely dries wall cavities. Drying decisions inside the walls are best left to the owner's chosen company.

Common tenant mistakes that weaken a claim

The most frequent mistake is throwing everything away at once. The second is ignoring a small drip for weeks, because slow leaks are exactly what many policies exclude. The third is signing a repair agreement or paying a contractor before knowing who is responsible.

A fourth mistake is skipping the mold check. TDI notes that mold can begin growing within a day after a leak, so a damp closet that smells musty deserves attention quickly. If it spreads, see our mold remediation service for how licensed professionals handle it.
