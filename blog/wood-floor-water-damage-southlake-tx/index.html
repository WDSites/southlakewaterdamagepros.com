Wood Floor Water Damage in Southlake, TX

What cupping, crowning, buckling and staining mean in the first days after a floor gets wet.

☎ Call (817) 623-9267

By the Southlake Water Damage Pros team · Updated 2026

Wood floor water damage shows up as warped boards, raised edges, dark stains or a musty smell after moisture gets under or into the planks. Homes in Southlake, TX can see it after a burst pipe, a failed washer hose or a slow air conditioner leak, and the first few days tell you a lot about what comes next.

This guide is organized by symptom. Find the sign you see on your floor, match it to a likely cause, and learn what to do today. Nothing here replaces a moisture reading, but it will help you act in the right order.

Common causes of wet wood floors

Most floor water damage comes from a short list of sources. Supply line failures under sinks, dishwashers and washing machines release steady water. Upstairs toilet and tub leaks drip into the floor system below. Air conditioner condensate lines that back up can wet a hallway floor slowly and quietly, which is a pattern we hear about from homeowners with cupped hardwood.

Weather matters too, in Southlake and in Keller alike. Freeze and thaw winters can burst pipes, and spring storms can overload gutters and push water toward doors. The February 2021 winter storm tested North Texas plumbing and water systems, and plenty of homeowners learned how fast a split line can soak a floor. Cupping in the first 48 hours

Cupping is the most common first sign, and it can appear within a day or two of a wet event. The bottom of each board absorbs more water than the top, so the board curls up at its edges. That shape tells you the moisture is coming from below or from a trapped source such as an under-floor leak.

Cupping from a short clean-water event often relaxes as the boards dry, which is why drying comes before sanding. If you sand cupped boards while they are still wet, they dry flat later and then crown. Our emergency water removal crews focus on pulling standing water first so the floor has a chance to settle.

Buckling and staining in the first week

Buckling is the dramatic one. Boards push up in a ridge or lift completely, and nails or glue let go. It usually follows a lot of water or several days of exposure, and it often means the subfloor is wet too.

Staining takes longer. Dark patches near nail heads or along seams come from water reacting with the wood and finish. Black stains that run deep into the wood do not sand out easily, and the board may be replaced. Early drying can limit how far staining spreads.

What to do in the first days

  • Find the source and stop it, starting with the main water valve if a supply line is leaking.
  • Turn off power to wet areas only if you can reach the panel dry. If electrical or gas risk is present, leave and call the utility or 911 first.
  • Soak up standing water with towels or a wet vacuum and lift rugs and furniture off the boards.
  • Keep people and pets out of any water that may be contaminated.
  • Photograph the floor, the source and any damaged items before cleaning.
  • Keep a fan moving air across the room, but do not cover the floor with plastic.

How a restoration crew confirms the damage

Crews measure before they decide. A moisture meter scans the planks and the subfloor, a thermal imaging camera traces cold wet zones behind the finish, and readings are compared with a dry reference board. Dehumidifiers and air movers go in once the wet area is mapped.

If the boards recover, the next step is refinishing, and if they do not, sections are replaced. We cover that path in more detail in the water damage repair scope, and the published ANSI/IICRC S500 standard lays out the drying principles, so ask any company whether its crews work to it.

When a wood floor problem is not an emergency

Not every floor change is a leak. Seasonal humidity swings can open small gaps and cause mild cupping in homes with no recent water event. If the pattern is uniform across the house and you have no moisture source, an HVAC or humidity issue is more likely.

Still, a floor that keeps cupping, a smell that will not leave, or any soft spot underfoot deserves a moisture reading. Better to learn the floor is dry than to cover a wet one with a new finish.
