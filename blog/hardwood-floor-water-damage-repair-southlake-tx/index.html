Hardwood Floor Water Damage Repair in Southlake, TX

How a wet hardwood floor is assessed, dried and then either refinished or replaced.

☎ Call (817) 623-9267

By the Southlake Water Damage Pros team · Updated 2026

Hardwood floor water damage repair is the process of drying wet boards and the layers under them, then deciding board by board whether the floor can be sanded and refinished or needs partial replacement. If you are standing over a swollen floor in Southlake, TX, the order of those steps matters more than the speed of the final finish coat.

Solid and engineered planks both absorb water through their edges and undersides, so what you see on top is only part of the story. A floor that looks fine on day two can still hold moisture in the subfloor, and that is what decides how the repair goes. The same logic applies for neighbors in Grapevine and Keller, where we also dispatch crews.

Start with an honest assessment of the floor

Before anyone picks a repair path, the floor needs to be read like a patient chart. Note where the water came from, how long it sat, and whether it was clean supply water, a washing machine overflow, or something dirtier such as a sewer backup. Dirtier water changes the plan because the boards and underlayment may have to go regardless of how they look.

Then look at the boards themselves. Raised edges that form a shallow trough are called cupping. A crown in the middle of a board is crowning. Boards that lift off the subfloor are buckling. Dark gray or black staining that runs through the wood usually points to water that sat for days, not hours.

What a moisture meter shows that your eyes cannot

A pin or pinless moisture meter reads the water content of the wood and the subfloor below it. Readings are compared with a dry reference board from an unaffected part of the same house, because the normal moisture level of a floor varies from home to home. A thermal imaging camera helps trace the wet area under the finish, but it points to cold spots and does not give a final moisture number.

This is why a visual check alone leads to bad calls. Finish can hide a wet subfloor, and a sanded floor sealed over a damp base is a floor that cups again in a few weeks. Readings get taken before drying starts, during it, and again before any refinishing.

Drying comes before any repair decision

Drying a hardwood floor means pulling moisture out slowly enough that the boards do not crack. Crews set air movers across the floor, run LGR dehumidifiers to keep the air dry, and often use a floor drying mat system that draws moisture up through the boards. Baseboards may be removed or drilled so air can reach the cavity behind the wall.

Most structural drying runs a few days, and thicker solid planks or wet subfloors can take longer. The target is a set of readings that match the dry reference, not a calendar date. Our water mitigation work follows this logic, and the industry standard ANSI/IICRC S500 describes it, so ask any company whether its crews work to it.

Sand and refinish, patch, or replace: a decision table

Once the floor is dry, the choice usually falls into one of three paths. This table shows the typical signs that point to each one. Final calls depend on moisture readings and a look at the fastening, so treat it as a guide and not a verdict.

What you see after dryingLikely pathWhy
Mild cupping that flattens as the floor driesSand and refinishThe boards are intact and settle back, so leveling the surface is enough
A few boards split, stained black or still raisedReplace those boards, then blendDamage is local, and new boards can be woven into the old pattern
Buckling across a large area or lifted nailingRemove and replace the sectionFasteners have let go and the subfloor often needs a look
Engineered planks with delaminated layersReplace the affected planksOnce the layers separate, drying will not rejoin them
Water from a sewer backup or other contaminated sourceRemove and replacePorous flooring that absorbed dirty water is not cleaned in place

Patch repairs can leave a color difference because old finish has aged and new boards have not. Sanding and refinishing the whole room, or at least to a natural break such as a doorway, is the usual way to make the result look even.

What refinishing and replacement actually involve

Refinishing means sanding off the old finish with a drum or orbital sander, filling small gaps, staining if needed, and applying coats of sealer that each need cure time. Rooms are off limits during the cure, so plan for furniture to stay out and for pets to stay clear. Dust containment keeps the mess from traveling through the house.

Replacement starts with removing damaged boards, checking and drying the subfloor, and installing new boards with matching species, width and grade. Matching old stock is the hard part, and sometimes boards pulled from a closet or hidden area supply the repair. Wherever the damage went beyond the floor, the work moves into water damage repair on walls and cabinets too.

Common mistakes that turn a dry floor into a replaced floor

  • Covering the wet floor with plastic or rugs, which traps moisture against the boards.
  • Sanding too early, before readings confirm the wood and subfloor are dry.
  • Using a household fan alone and calling it done, when the subfloor is still wet.
  • Waiting several days to start drying, which gives staining and mold a head start.
  • Pulling up all the boards at once when only a section needed to come out.

A good test is simple. If someone wants to refinish before showing you dry readings, ask for the numbers first.

Insurance notes for a wet hardwood floor

The Texas Department of Insurance explains that most homeowner and renters policies cover sudden and accidental water damage, such as a burst pipe or a broken washer hose. Gradual leaks and seepage are usually not covered, and neither is flooding unless you carry a separate flood policy.

Take photos or video of the floor before repairs, keep damaged boards until the adjuster has seen them, and make temporary repairs to stop further damage. Hold off on permanent repairs until the insurer inspects, and keep your receipts. Nobody can promise that a claim will be approved, so ask your insurer what your policy says.
