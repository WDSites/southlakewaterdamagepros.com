Stop the spread, find the hidden moisture, and start drying before a small leak becomes a rebuild.

Water mitigation is the work done in the first 24 to 72 hours to stop water from spreading and to start drying the structure. It is the stage that decides whether you end up with a wet carpet or a gutted wall. Everything after it, from cleanup to rebuilding and repair, depends on how well this stage goes.

Wet drywall, a musty smell, soft baseboards, or carpet that squishes?

Those four signs mean water has gone past the visible spill. Drywall that feels cool and damp is wicking moisture up from a wet floor line. Baseboards that have gone soft are holding water against the wall. Carpet that squishes means the pad underneath is saturated, and a musty smell tells you the cavity behind the wall is staying wet.

The trade name for the fix is water mitigation. Crews stop the source, find every wet surface with moisture meters and thermal imaging cameras, pull out standing water, and set drying equipment before the damage travels. A thermal camera shows cold patches that often mark wet insulation, and a meter then confirms the reading inside the material itself.

That pairing replaces guessing. It keeps crews from cutting open walls that are actually dry, and it keeps them from leaving wet framing behind a wall that only looks fine.

Water is still spreading through your home right now

Water travels. It runs under flooring, into wall cavities, and down through ceilings into the room below. The Texas Department of Insurance notes that mold can start growing within a day after a leak, so the first 24 hours set the size of the whole job.

Typical starting points include an upstairs toilet leak that stains the ceiling below, a washer supply valve that fails while no one is home, and an AC condensate line that backs up and cups hardwood. The first move is the same each time. Stop the source, then call dispatch so drying can begin.

  • Close the main water shut-off valve, or the fixture valve if the leak is local.
  • Switch off power to wet rooms only if the panel is dry and easy to reach.
  • Lift rugs, electronics, and small furniture off wet floors.
  • Take photos or video of the damage before anything gets moved.
  • Keep people and pets out of water that may be contaminated.

Electrical and sewage risk before anyone steps in

If you smell gas, see sparking outlets, or find water around the electrical panel, leave the house and call 911 or the utility before you call us. Water and live circuits do not mix, and no belongings are worth the risk.

Raw sewage from a lower-level drain is a different situation. Stay out of that water, keep children and pets away, and tell dispatch when you call so the crew arrives with the right containment plan.

Structural drying

Structural drying is the longest part of mitigation, and it is more than running fans. Air movers push dry air across wet surfaces so moisture leaves the material. LGR dehumidifiers, short for low grain refrigerant units, pull that moisture out of the air so it cannot settle back into drywall, framing, or subfloor. The two work as a pair, and neither one dries a house alone.

Crews take moisture readings on day one and again at each visit. They compare every reading to a dry reference, which is a reading from the same material in an unaffected part of the home. Drying continues until the wet materials match that reference, not until the room simply feels dry. Pulling equipment early is how hidden dampness ends up behind a freshly painted wall.

Dense materials such as hardwood, framing, and subfloor release water more slowly than drywall, so equipment gets moved, added, or removed as the numbers change. ANSI/IICRC S500 is the published industry standard for this work, and you can ask any company whether its crews work to it.
