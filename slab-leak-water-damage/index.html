A slab leak is a break or pinhole in a water line that runs in, under or through a concrete foundation slab. This page applies only if your house was built on a slab, and plenty of Southlake homes sit on other foundation types, so check before you worry. Once a plumber stops the leak, our crews handle the wet flooring, walls and baseboards that the water reached.

Warm floor, running sounds, or a water bill that jumped?

Slab leaks give clues long before a puddle appears. A pressurized line that leaks under concrete pushes water sideways until it finds a crack, a joint or the edge of the slab. By then the moisture has often been working on flooring and wall bases for a while.

The table below pairs each common sign with what it usually means. None of these signs proves a slab leak by itself, so a plumber with leak detection gear has to confirm the location before anyone cuts concrete or flooring.

Water is coming up through your floor right now

Closing the main water valve is the single best thing you can do. It stops new water from feeding the leak, and it lets the slab begin to shed moisture instead of absorbing more. If the leak is on the hot side, switching off the water heater at its own shutoff also limits further flow.

First-hour safety steps before anyone calls a crew

Water that has been under a slab can wick into baseboards, door jambs and the bottom of electrical outlets. Treat any wet outlet or wet panel area as live.

  • Shut off the main water valve and, if you know how, the water heater's supply.
  • Turn off power to wet rooms at the breaker only if the panel is dry and you can reach it without stepping in water.
  • If you smell gas, leave the house and call 911 or the gas utility before you call us.
  • Call a licensed plumber for leak detection, because we dry and restore but do not repair pipes.
  • For water on the street side of the meter, call Southlake Public Works at (817) 748-8082.

Who fixes what: your plumber, the city, and our crew

The city's own website says homeowners are responsible for the service line from the house to and including the tap, while the city repairs any part of that line that lies under the road. That split matters because a leak on the street side may not be your repair at all. The city also asks residents to contact its Wastewater Division before calling a plumber about service line problems.

For a leak inside the house footprint, a licensed plumber finds it with acoustic and thermal tools, then chooses between a spot repair, rerouting the line through walls or attic, or replacing sections. The City of Southlake permit guide lists replacing existing water or sewer lines as work that needs a permit, so ask your plumber who pulls it, or call the building inspections office at (817) 748-8236.

How our crews dry a slab-on-grade home

Concrete holds water like a sponge, so the drying plan starts with moisture mapping. Crews use moisture meters and thermal imaging cameras to trace how far the water traveled from the leak, across the slab and into wall bases. Wet carpet pad and baseboards usually come out first because they trap water against the slab.

Next come air movers and LGR dehumidifiers sized to the wet area, plus floor drying mats or panels that pull moisture out of hard flooring. If the plumber opens the slab to reach the pipe, we coordinate timing so drying equipment is not running while dust is flying. Crews log readings at each visit until the slab and framing reach their dry standard.

Our general approach follows the same stages as water mitigation, and the deeper drying work is covered on the structural drying page. Ask any company whether its crews work to the published ANSI/IICRC S500 standard for water damage restoration.

How insurance usually fits a leak under the house

A Texas Department of Insurance bulletin on foundations states the agency's position that the standard Texas homeowner form covers dwelling damage from accidental discharge of water from plumbing, including a leak beneath the house. Policies differ, and some carry a tear-out limitation, so ask your insurer how your policy treats the pipe access and the foundation.

Most policies cover sudden and accidental water, and many do not cover slow seepage that went on for a long time. Check your policy, report the leak promptly, photograph everything before repairs and keep receipts. We never promise approval, and the decision belongs to your insurer.

Mistakes to avoid with a suspected slab leak

  • Waiting to see whether the bill drops, which lets water keep feeding the slab.
  • Running a heater or fan on a warm spot, which can hide the source and push moisture into walls.
  • Sealing or tiling over damp flooring before it is measured dry.
  • Letting someone cut the slab without a leak detection report and a plan for the permit.
  • Throwing out wet materials before the adjuster has seen them.
